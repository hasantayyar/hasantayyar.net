### Oak tree is edible apparently

Berlin is home to famous historical specimens, such as the *Dicke Marie* in the Tegel Forest, an English oak estimated to be around 800 years old. Older than the Berlin city itself. It’s a young city in Europe at the end.

Oak trees yield edible parts, but **none can be eaten raw** due to toxic tannins, which cause severe digestive distress. They require proper processing first.

- **Acorns (Nut):** High in fats and starches. Must be shelled and leached in multiple changes of water until the bitterness is gone. Processed acorns can be roasted, mashed into porridge, or ground into gluten-free flour.
- **Young Leaves:** Lower in tannins when pale green in spring, but still bitter and tough. Historically used for survival or herbal tea rather than as food.
- **Inner Bark:** Inedible as food. High tannin levels make it strictly medicinal (used as an astringent gargle or tea for diarrhea) or for leather tanning; ingesting it irritates the digestive tract.

## Prediction, language, and Markov chains

I revisited Noam Chomsky’s 2012 critique of statistical AI. 

https://chomsky.info/20121101/

Chomsky's 2012 reflection was prescient. While the statistical approach to AI achieved a level of commercial and practical success far beyond what many expected a decade ago, its core limitation persists: **prediction is not comprehension**. In both scientific inquiry and geopolitical implementation, treating statistical correlation as a substitute for deep structural understanding creates systems that are powerful, yet fragile and opaque.

That led naturally to **Andrey Markov**. In 1913, he analysed the first 20,000 letters of Pushkin’s *Eugene Onegin*, classifying them as vowels or consonants and measuring how one category followed another. It became the first empirical application of what we now call Markov chains. The Veritasium video is a good introduction.

> In 1913, Andrey Markov conducted a study on the distribution of vowels and consonants in the first 20,000 letters of Alexander Pushkin's *Eugene Onegin*, treating the text as a sequence of symbols and analyzing the statistical relationships between them. By classifying each letter as either a vowel or a consonant and analyzing the probabilities of transitions between these categories, Markov demonstrated that chains of dependent events could be rigorously modeled. This was the first empirical application of what are now called Markov chains.
 

The same idea appears in Google’s original PageRank: think of pages as states and links as transitions, then find the long-run probability of a random surfer landing on each page. A small interactive workbook made the connection concrete.

> Noam Chomsky, a highly respected linguist, joined with two colleagues in a critique of LLMs. In addition to the issues previously discussed, they noted that LLMs’ method of learning language-scraping huge text databases from the internet-is completely different from the way children learn language. We acquire language by being immersed in the conversations and practices of human communities. This means there is ultimately no way LLMs can learn language in the way humans learn and live language-no way they can overcome the problems mentioned earlier. After iterating with ChatGPT on questions around morality and ethics, Chomsky
concluded:
ChatGPT exhibits something like the banality of evil: plagiarism and apathy and obviation. It summarizes the standard arguments in the literature by a kind of super-autocomplete, refuses to take a stand on anything, pleads not merely ignorance but lack of intelligence and ultimately offers a “just following orders” defense, shifting responsibility to its creators. Chomsky worried that with all the enthusiasm to incorporate LLMs into many computer applications, we will infuse this technology into many other technologies, a situation we might regret and be unable to turn off


Quotes from https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/3747356

## Martial arts: Taido and Keysi

I started reading about **Taido**, a Japanese martial art founded by Seiken Shukumine in 1965. Compared with karate, its movement language feels more focused on mobility, off-axis attacks, spins, rolls, and changing levels. Instead of treating a stable stance as the default, it often turns the body into a moving target.

I also read about the Keysi Fighting Method, a close-range self-defence system built around survival in crowded, unpredictable situations. Its signature *Pensador* position uses the hands and elbows to protect the head and neck, while elbows, hammer fists, knees, and constant pivots become the main tools. Its visual influence is easy to spot in Christopher Nolan’s Batman films.

First Seiken Shukumine Genseiryu karate founded in 1950, but in the 1960's he found that the flow of martial arts, he was far away from karate, so he founded the new martial view the name of Taido

Quote from  https://j2n-ma.blogspot.com/2012/08/taido.html

Key Technical Divergences:
- Movement Dynamics & Axis of Attack
  - Karate: Focuses on generating maximum power from a stable connection to the ground. Energy flows through the legs, hips, and core into strikes via solid, rooted stances.
  - Taido: Treats the human body like a gyroscope. Strikes are delivered mid-flight, mid-roll, or while bending the spine off-axis. A technique is rarely executed from a static upright position.
- Tactical Philosophy & Space Creation
  - Karate: Employs precise distance management (Ma-ai) to intercept attacks, block-counter instantly, or absorb pressure while maintaining control of the line of engagement.
  - Taido: Rejects static defense. Instead of blocking a blow, a Taido practitioner drops their torso to the mat or cartwheels sideways, using the attacker's momentum against them while striking from a blind spot.

I also learned about **Keysi Fighting Method (KFM)**. It is a self-defense system developed in Spain **during the 1950s and 1990s** by Justo Diéguez and Andy Norman. Built around street survival rather than sport, it assumes you are fighting multiple attackers at close range, often in tight spaces with no rules.

Unlike martial arts that rely on maintaining distance or standing long guards, KFM brings the fight into extremely close quarters (*ultra-close range*).

## Read this paper and like: "The AI layoff trap"

[https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.20617](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.20617)

> If AI displaces human workers faster than the economy can reabsorb them, it risks eroding
> the very consumer demand firms depend on. We show that knowing this is not enough for firms
> to stop it. In a competitive task-based model of a transitioning economy, each firm captures
> the full cost saving from automation but bears only a fraction of the demand loss it creates
> in the product market; the rest falls on rivals. This demand externality traps rational firms
> in an automation arms race, displacing workers well beyond what is collectively optimal. The
> resulting loss harms both workers and firm owners. More competition and “better” AI amplify
> the excess; wage adjustments and free entry cannot eliminate it. Neither can capital income
> taxes, worker equity, universal basic income, upskilling, or Coasean bargaining. A Pigouvian
> automation tax can. The results suggest that policy should address not only the aftermath of
> AI labor displacement but also the competitive incentives that drive it.

## Masha Ivashintsova (1942–2000) - a Soviet photographer recently revealed extensive photo archive

[https://mashaivashintsova.com/](https://mashaivashintsova.com/)


Masha Ivashintsova (1942–2000) was a Soviet photographer whose extensive archive of over 30,000 negatives remained completely undiscovered until 2017. Often referred to as the "Russian Vivian Maier," she spent decades capturing everyday life, political atmosphere, and the artistic underground of Leningrad (St. Petersburg) without ever showing her work to the public.  
• **Underground Artistic Circle:** Ivashintsova was heavily immersed in Leningrad’s non-conformist movement between the 1960s and 1980s. She had close relationships with prominent figures of the Soviet underground, including poet Viktor Krivulin, photographer Boris Smelov, and painter Boris Akselrod.
• **Involuntary Commitment:** Because she did not maintain official state-sanctioned employment and lived outside Soviet societal expectations, she was subjected to punitive psychiatry and involuntarily committed to a Soviet mental hospital for ten years (1981–1991).  
• **Discovery of Her Archive:** In late 2017, 17 years after her death from cancer, her daughter Asya Ivashintsova-Melkumyan discovered box after box of undeveloped rolls and film negatives in an attic.
• **Photographic Style:** Her work features candid street photography, raw urban landscapes of Leningrad, intimate portraits of underground figures, and melancholy self-portraits. She shot predominantly on Leica and Rolleiflex cameras.
